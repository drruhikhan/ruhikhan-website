A landmark new report from UN Women, Tipping Point: Online Violence — Impacts, Manifestations and Redress in the AI Age, confirms what feminist researchers and journalists have been documenting for years: AI is not a neutral technology, and women in public life are paying the price for treating it as though it were. Based on a global survey of 641 women journalists, activists and human rights defenders across 119 countries, the findings are stark—forty-one per cent self-censor on social media to avoid abuse. Police reports have doubled since 2020, yet only 15% result in any legal action. A quarter of those who did report were made to feel responsible for the violence against them.

 

This is the terrain my forthcoming book, Equivocal Feminism (Oxford University Press), was written to map. Using case studies, I show that digital platforms simultaneously amplified women's voices and made them targets — a paradox of visibility and vulnerability that the UN Women data now confirms at a global scale. AI has not created this dynamic. It has industrialised it. Deepfake tools, nudification apps, and coordinated harassment campaigns are the latest expressions of what I call media and tech coloniality: the embedding of existing power hierarchies into the infrastructures that govern who gets to speak, be heard, and remain safe in public life.

 

"Virtual Rape" Is Not a Metaphor

 

The report's lead author, Professor Julie Posetti of City, St George's, University of London, called this: "AI-assisted 'virtual rape.'" She describes it as now being "at the fingertips of perpetrators," accelerating the harm inflicted on women in public life. I want to sit with that phrase, because our collective instinct, particularly in mainstream discourse, is to reach for softer language. "Image-based abuse." "Non-consensual intimate imagery." These terms are accurate, but they can inadvertently distance us from the visceral reality of what is happening.

 

When someone fabricates a sexual image of you without your consent and distributes it, whether it is a photograph, a video, or an AI-generated deepfake, they are committing an act of sexual violation. The fact that your body was not physically touched does not make the violation less real. The psychological harm, the reputational damage, the loss of bodily autonomy: it is all present. The report documents this clearly. Nearly a quarter of women journalists surveyed (24.7%) have been diagnosed with anxiety or depression connected to the online violence they have experienced, while almost 13% reported post-traumatic stress disorder. These are clinical diagnoses. This is not women being oversensitive to the rough-and-tumble of public life. This is a mental health crisis with a clear and traceable cause.

 

The report arrives at a moment of significant regulatory rollback, democratic backsliding, and networked misogyny. The institutional failures it documents, from victim-blaming police responses to platform inaction, are not incidental. They reflect a systemic choice about whose safety counts. As a social scientist, journalist and feminist, I read this report not as a revelation but as a reckoning: the evidence has been building for years, and the question now is whether policymakers and platforms will act on it. 

 

Now comes the harder part: deciding, as societies, whether we believe women's participation in public life is worth protecting. I believe it is. The evidence demands that we act accordingly.
