About

Lecturer, Researcher, Journalist

Dr Ruhi Khan, FRSA, FHEA, is a lecturer, researcher and journalist at the London School of Economics whose work moves between the academy, the newsroom and the policy world.

As a lecturer, she has taught across LSE's Department of Media and Communications, Department of Sociology and Department of Gender Studies over the past six years. She is committed to inclusive pedagogy, interdisciplinary curriculum design and the mentorship of the next generation of researchers and communicators. She has also given invited talks at the University of Cambridge, Imperial College London, SOAS and Royal Holloway, University of London.

As a researcher, she works at the intersection of AI ethics, tech governance, digital feminism and the political economy of media — with sustained focus on postcolonial societies and the Global South. She is currently a Research Officer on the AHRC-funded project Crafting Sustainability and Equitability: Reconstructing Pasts and Futures in the Indian Creative Economy at LSE's Department of Gender Studies, and a faculty affiliate of the LSE Data Science Institute. Her ESRC-funded doctoral research produced an original theoretical contribution — the conceptualisation of "equivocal agency" — the basis of her forthcoming monograph Equivocal Feminism: Media, Technology and the Global Digital Order (Oxford University Press).

As a journalist and editor, she brings over 15 years of international reporting experience across Europe, Asia and North America — covering AI summits, elections, conflicts, COPs and major policy events. As Editor of the LSE Media Blog for six years, she translated complex scholarship into public dialogue, policy insight and global readership engagement. She is co-author of Escaped: Indian Fugitives in London (Penguin Random House, 2021). This book was widely covered in the press in India and across the world. Several of the interviews, podcasts and talks can be found here.

UK–India Relations March 2026

On the UK-India AI Working Lunch: What a seat at the table really means

For a long time, the conversation around AI felt like a race — to be first, biggest, most profitable. So it was striking to sit in the High Commission of India in London, for a dialogue on AI governance, and find the room wasn't talking about compute power. It was talking about consent. Rishi Sunak reframed the UK-India relationship as a partnership of shared values, not a technology transaction. Dame Wendy Hall made the case for a third way — beyond the US-China duopoly — built on the genuine complementarity of British and Indian strengths. Jade Leung, AI adviser to the UK Prime Minister, was direct: building AI for public services without public consent isn't innovation, it's imposition. Pushmeet Kohli of Google DeepMind reminded the room that democratising intelligence means bringing people along, not just delivering technology to them. The thread running through all of it was a single word: trust.

This matters to me because ethical AI has been at the core of my research long before it became a conference talking point. My work at LSE keeps returning to the same conviction: the best version of AI isn't the fastest one — it's the most inclusive one. AI must stop being built for the Global South and start being built with it. The Global South is not a market to be served. It is a partner in shaping what comes next. That is the UK-India relationship I want to see — not a tech transaction, but a genuine exchange of values, capacity and trust. I left that lunch with something rarer than a business card: genuine optimism.

AI Ethics & Governance April 2024

Patriarchal AI is not a bug — it is a feature of how these systems are built

When The Telegraph ran my research showing that ChatGPT consistently represents CEOs as men — and professionals in positions of authority as male, white and Western — the response from some quarters was: "it's just reflecting reality." This is precisely the problem. AI systems do not neutrally describe the world. They are trained on historical data that encodes centuries of structural inequality, and when they reproduce those patterns they do not merely reflect reality — they consecrate it. They lend the inequalities of the past the authority of the algorithmic present, making them appear natural, inevitable and objective. That is not reflection. That is reproduction at scale.

The deeper issue is one of design. These systems are built by a remarkably homogeneous group — predominantly male, predominantly from the Global North, trained in technical disciplines that have historically treated social context as peripheral. The result is not malice but structurally predictable: systems that encode the worldview of their makers, then present that worldview as universal truth. What feminist AI scholarship makes clear is that patriarchal AI is not an anomaly to be patched with better prompts or bias audits. It is a consequence of who builds these systems, what data they are trained on, and whose harms are treated as edge cases. Fixing it requires structural transformation — diverse teams, feminist data governance, and a genuine commitment to co-creation with the communities most affected.
